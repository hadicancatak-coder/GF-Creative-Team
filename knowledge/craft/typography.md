# Typography for ads

An ad is not a page. Type here is a **visual element first** and a reading experience second.

## Scale — the decision most often ducked
A display size inside the scale is not thereby the right size. **The top step exists to be spent.**

- **Statement ads should be at the top step.** If the line is the ad, set it like it is the ad.
- **Scale contrast must be real: 3× minimum** between display and body. 2× reads as an accident — the
  viewer cannot tell whether the hierarchy is deliberate.
- Work out what your display size becomes at thumbnail: `size × 110 / canvas_height`. Under ~8px the
  words do not survive, and you need either a bigger step or a non-verbal carrier.

## Setting display type
- **Line-height tightens as size grows.** Body 1.4–1.5; display 0.9–1.05. Display set at 1.4 falls apart
  into separate lines with no relationship.
- **Tracking tightens as size grows.** Body 0; display −1% to −3%. Large type set at default tracking
  looks loose and amateur.
- **Break on meaning, never on measure.** Set the breaks by hand. `Nothing miraculous / happens.` is
  wrong; `Nothing miraculous happens. / That's the product.` is right. The break is a beat.
- **Left-align display type.** Centred display reads as a quote card or a slide. Centre it only when the
  whole composition is on an axis and that symmetry means something.
- Two lines is the norm. Three when the third lands a turn. Four is a paragraph, and a paragraph is not
  a headline.

## Pairing
- **One family is a legitimate choice**, and often the better one — use weight and size for hierarchy.
- **Two families must be clearly distinct.** A serif and a sans, or a display and a text face. Two
  similar sans-serifs read as a mistake, not a pairing.
- Never three. If you think you need three, one of them is decoration.

## Measure
- **45–75 characters per line** for body copy. Over 80 the eye loses the return.
- Serif tolerates slightly longer lines; sans wants slightly shorter, with more line-height.
- Legal and mandated lines get the **same care as everything else** — at spec size, adjacent to the claim
  they qualify, with real contrast. A disclaimer set to be unreadable is a compliance risk, not a design
  decision.

## Contrast — check it, do not eyeball it
- Body text: **4.5:1** minimum. Large text (≥24px or ≥19px bold): **3:1**.
- **Knockout text on an accent colour is where this usually fails.** Light-on-mid-tone frequently lands
  around 2.5–3:1. Compute it; do not assume the brand's ink works on the brand's accent.
- Muted greys on dark grounds: verify. "Muted" often means "below 4.5:1".

## The tells — treatments that mark work as generated
Avoid unless the brief explicitly calls for them:

- **Accenting one word** of a headline in italic, bold or a different colour.
- **ALL-CAPS tracked-out eyebrows** above a heading. Especially above every heading.
- A **label above content** that the content already explains.
- **`01 / 02 / 03` markers** on content that is not actually a sequence.
- **`→` appended** to button or link text.
- **Monospace for small data labels** where nothing is code.
- **Meta strings joined with middle dots** — `A · B · C`.
- **`WORD — fragment`** with a spaced em dash as a label pattern.
- **Tinted near-black** (`#0B0B0B`, `#111`) standing in for black.

Each is legitimate somewhere. The problem is that they appear regardless of subject, which means they
were never chosen.

## Before you sign off
1. Read the **first three words**. Would a stranger stop?
2. Is the headline a **turn** or a **description**? A description tells someone who already wants the
   product which one to buy, and does nothing for the 99% scrolling past.
3. Did you **spend the top of the scale**, or hedge to the middle?
4. Cover the image. Does the type still carry the idea?
5. Cover the type. Does anything remain?
