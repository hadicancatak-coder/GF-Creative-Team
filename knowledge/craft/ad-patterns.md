# Ad patterns — the structures that actually work in a feed

Not a style guide. These are the **load-bearing structures** an ad can have. Pick one deliberately and
build it properly; most bad ads are two of these fighting each other in the same frame.

A feed ad gets **0.5 seconds** in a scroll. In that time a viewer resolves exactly one thing. The
pattern decides what that one thing is.

---

## 1. THE OBJECT
The product, shot or rendered, owning 50–70% of the frame. Everything else serves it.

**Works when** the product is visually distinctive, or desire is physical — food, drink, apparel,
hardware, cosmetics.
**Fails when** the product is a box, an app, or a service. A generic box shot under any headline is the
single most common waste of a feed slot.
**Gets it right:** one light source, one angle, contact with a surface (floating products look fake),
and crop *into* the object rather than centring it politely on a white field.

## 2. THE NUMBER
One figure at display scale, doing the persuading on its own. `£4/month`. `200mg`. `48 hours`.

**Works when** you have a genuinely strong number and the category is comparison-shopped.
**Fails when** the number needs a sentence to be impressive. If it needs context, it is not the hero.
**Gets it right:** the number is 4–8× the size of everything else. Not 2×. The unit is small and set
tight to the numeral. **Never centre it** — a big number centred on a field reads as a slide, not an ad.

## 3. THE STATEMENT
Type only. The line *is* the ad.

**Works when** the proposition is verbal, counter-intuitive, or the brand is the point. Especially for
categories where the product photographs badly, or where no photography exists yet.
**Fails when** the line is a description rather than a turn. "Premium magnesium supplement" is a label.
"Nothing miraculous happens." is a turn.
**Gets it right:** **spend the top of your type scale.** A statement ad set at the middle step is a
statement ad that has lost its nerve. Break lines on *meaning*, never on measure. Two lines usually; three
if the third lands a turn. Left-align — centred display type reads as a quote card.

## 4. THE COMPARISON
Two states in one frame. Before/after, with/without, them/us.

**Works when** the difference is visible in a thumbnail.
**Fails when** the two states look similar at 110px, which is most of the time. Test it before you build.
**Gets it right:** a hard division — no soft gradient between the halves — and the *same* framing on both
sides so the difference is the only variable. Label both, minimally.

## 5. THE DEMONSTRATION
The product mid-use. A hand, a pour, a wear, a moment of function.

**Works when** the benefit is a verb. It is the strongest pattern in the list when you can afford it,
because it shows rather than claims and sidesteps most claim-substantiation problems.
**Fails when** you have no such asset — and it is the pattern people most often try to fake. Do not.

## 6. THE SYSTEM
A grid, set, or repetition. Many of one thing.

**Works when** range, variety or quantity *is* the proposition. Colourways, a menu, a library, a count.
**Fails when** repetition is used decoratively. Thirty marks that mean "thirty servings" is a system; a
pattern that means nothing is wallpaper, and it will eat frame space the message needs.
**Gets it right:** the repetition must be **countable or obviously uncountable** — never ambiguous.
Twelve looks like a mistake; either 3–7 (countable at a glance) or 40+ (reads as "many").

## 7. THE SURFACE
The frame is a texture, material or colour field. Brand-led, product-absent.

**Works when** the brand already has recognition, or the category is mood-led.
**Fails when** nobody knows who you are. A mood with no name is somebody else's ad.

---

## Choosing

Ask two questions, in order:

1. **What does the viewer have to believe?** Then: does that belief arrive faster through an object, a
   number, a sentence, a contrast, a demonstration, a set, or a mood?
2. **What assets actually exist?** A pattern you cannot source is not a pattern, it is a wish. No
   photography means patterns 1, 4, 5 and 7 are usually off the table — say so and choose from what is
   left rather than faking one.

## Anti-patterns — the ones that read as advertising nobody chose

- **The sandwich.** Logo top, product middle, CTA bottom, all centred, even margins. It is not a
  composition, it is a form. Nothing owns the frame.
- **Two heroes.** A big product *and* a big headline, both fighting. Decide which one is the ad.
- **The caption problem.** A frame that only makes sense once you read the caption. The frame ships
  alone into screenshots, reshares and Stories chrome — it must stand up on its own.
- **Decoration outweighing the message.** If the thing occupying most of the frame is not the thing
  doing the persuading, the ad is backwards.
- **Symmetry by default.** Centred everything is the safest and least memorable choice available.
